Friction

  • Three rows compete for attention. No row reads as primary.
  • Overlapping IA: Parts appears twice (Order Parts + Parts), Service appears twice (Request Service + Service).
  • Yellow utility strip + yellow phone strip both fight for the eye.
  • Mixed audiences: Login (customer) + Employee Login (internal staff) in the same row.
  • Mobile collapse hides 2 of 3 rows — utility + mega vanish at ≤768px.
  • Mega-row labels (Quotes / Attachments / Power Systems) demand the user already know dealer-industry catalog vocabulary.

What it does well

  • Internal users can deep-link to every tool (Vision Link, SIS 2.0, Rental Portal) in one click.
  • Phone number visible above the fold for service-call audience.
  • Yellow utility strip signals chain-identity (Cat dealer pattern).

Cost

  • Scroll-blocking header height: ~140px desktop. Hero pushed below the fold.
  • First-time visitor has to scan 25 labels to decide where to click.
  • Brand "primary action" unclear: rent? buy? call? login? all equally weighted.
Pattern A · Audience verbs

Four big intent-verbs. Hover reveals depth.

The page asks "why are you here?" first. Picks one of four answers: BUY / RENT / SERVICE / PARTS. Everything else is one click deeper.

Pros

  • 4 visible primary items (down from 25). 6× less to scan.
  • Verbs match how users actually arrive: "I want to rent something" → click RENT.
  • Resolves the overlap problem: Parts.Cat.com / SIS 2.0 / Vision Link live under PARTS where they belong.
  • Phone CTA gets exclusive yellow — primary action is unambiguous.
  • Cleaner aesthetic: white ground, dark text, yellow accent only on hover + CTA.

Cons

  • Hover dropdowns invisible to touch users until tapped (need explicit tap-to-open on mobile).
  • Loses the always-visible utility strip — Login is one click away instead of zero.
  • Verb labels are editorial choices: BUY vs SHOP vs PURCHASE matters for SEO.
  • Internal-staff tools (Employee Login) move from chrome into a footer or login page.

Best fit

  • When the brand wants a confident, opinionated presentation of "here's what we do."
  • When the primary funnel is a clear customer action (not internal-tool deep-link).
  • Wheeler-as-presales: this is what a brand-team mock should look like.

Pros

  • Familiar pattern — users have learned this on Cat OEM, Komatsu, John Deere dealer sites.
  • Six visible items + one CTA. Lower scan cost than current but more depth than Pattern A.
  • Mega-panel can group items under sub-headings (Buy / Rent / Schedule / Programs).
  • Persistent yellow CTA ("Request a Quote") gives the brand one obvious next-step regardless of intent.
  • Light chrome — header is ~72px tall, less aggressive than current 140px.

Cons

  • Hover-only menus are a touch-device tax (need click-to-open on mobile).
  • "Equipment" lumps BUY + RENT into one menu — slightly less direct than Pattern A's 4 verbs.
  • Six items + CTA still requires medium-width viewports; tablet portrait will tighten.

Best fit

  • When the brand wants industry-conformant familiarity, not a contrarian statement.
  • When the catalog depth (sub-categories per primary) genuinely earns mega-panels.
  • When the persistent CTA ("Request a Quote") is a real conversion goal.
Pattern C · Drawer-first

Minimal top bar. Hamburger always visible. Nav lives in a drawer.

Borrowed from premium / editorial sites (Apple, NYT, OEM corporate). Top bar shows only what matters above the fold: logo, search, account, phone. Full nav is opt-in via a left-drawer that respects every level of depth without faking flatness.

Pros

  • 4 visible chrome items: burger, logo, account, phone. Plus a search bar.
  • Drawer accommodates every item from the current 25 without compromise — grouped honestly under headings.
  • Search bar in the top bar serves the "I want a 259D3" / "branch in Cedar City" intent in one keystroke.
  • Touch parity: hamburger behaves identically on desktop and mobile.
  • Header is ~72px and stays that height at every viewport.

Cons

  • Hides the IA. First-time visitors don't see what categories exist without clicking.
  • SEO loses always-visible inline links (a hamburger is a single anchor, not 25).
  • Contrarian for an industrial dealer — Caterpillar OEM and most peer dealers use mega-menu Pattern B. Risk of feeling "too tech-startup."
  • Two clicks instead of one for top destinations (open drawer → click target).

Best fit

  • When search is the primary discovery mechanism (users arriving with a model code in mind).
  • When the brand wants a premium / editorial register over a "browse the catalog" register.
  • When the page itself does the wayfinding (hero CTAs, service tiles, blog cards) — the header just gets out of the way.

Recommendation

For a presales redesign that needs to read as "the version Wheeler should be tomorrow", Pattern A (audience verbs) is the strongest opinion. It re-asks the IA question Wheeler's current site avoids and gives the brand four confident answers. Pattern B is the safe industry-conformant fallback. Pattern C is the bold contrarian play (good if the brand wants to feel un-dealer-like).
